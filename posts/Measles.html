Where's The Herd Heading?

A 2025 Look Into the Comeback of Measles, The Disease Thought Eradicated In 2000

Posted by Angel Alberto Aldaco on November 15, 2025

Despite being declared eliminated in the United States in 2000, Measles has seen a large uptick in cases 25 years later, with a major outbreak this year in Gaines County, Texas. Conjointly, vaccination rates, attributed to eradicating the disease in 2000, have sharply dropped across the country. This has come at what medical experts view to be a great loss, as communities across America lose “Herd Immunity”, a threshold of vaccinated rates to ensure that the general population is protected from the disease. In the case of Measles, it requires the community to achieve a 95% vaccination rate.

Though herd immunity through strong vaccination rates is what eradicated the disease in 2000, there has been an uptick in MMR vaccine skepticism in recent years. According to a Kaiser Family Foundation Report, in 2025, around 1 in 6 parents (17%) reported delaying or skipping shots, whereas in 2023, it was around 10% of parents. Note that in order to maintain herd immunity, only 5% of the population, or 1 in 20 people, can afford to be unimmunized.

Similarly, in 2023, only a third of adults ruled the claims that MMR vaccines cause autism are “definitely false”, while the remaining population was open to claims that vaccines cause autism. These well-refuted claims originally stemmed from a now-declared fraudulent 1998 scientific paper by Andrew Wakefield, whom Health and Human Services (HHS) Secretary Robert F Kennedy Jr. has defended. Mr. Wakefield’s paper was retracted after the editor’s investigations found its statements to be “utterly false”.

A more thorough way to look at these changes in vaccination rates would be to go county by county. Take, as an example, the larger state of Texas. The lack of Herd Immunity in Gaines County, Texas, is less likely to hurt the community of Webb County, Texas, which is across the state and has obtained Herd Immunity. Upon using that metric in analysis, we still have a larger number of counties that sway against vaccinations.

(Note: This is ultimately an incomplete picture as many counties do not have reportable numbers)

Gaines County, Texas, in March of this year, faced a historic outbreak of measles, climbing to 414 cases at the time of reporting. Tragically, the outbreak has led to the first recorded death of Measles in the past 10 years. According to the latest data, the county did not have herd immunity at an 81.92% vaccination rate, falling short by more than 10%. In a larger analysis of counties that report vaccination data, only 700 out of the more than 3100 counties meet the 95% threshold for herd immunity. This is additionally not isolated to Gaines County, as at least 202 counties have a similar or worse vaccination rate.

In responding to the crisis, HHS Secretary RFK Jr wrote an op-ed in Fox News that “Vaccines not only protect individual children from measles, but contribute to community immunity”. Concerning medical experts, however, was his promotion of good nutrition and vitamin A as “the best defense against infectious disease” rather than vaccinations. While experts such as Dr. Adam Ratner of the infectious disease committee of the American Academy of Pediatrics acknowledge that nutrition and vitamin A aid the prevention of severe infectious disease, it doesn’t replace that “the way to deal with a measles outbreak is to vaccinate people against measles”.

This is especially alarming when the Health Secretary has been a polarizing figure for a medically polarized public. Again, according to a study by the Kaiser Family Foundation, in January of 2025, 33% of Republicans said that the risk of childhood vaccines for Measles, Mumps, and Rubella outweighs the benefits. To place that in perspective, in the same time frame, only 15% of Democrats said the same. Medical experts agree that vaccination remains the safest and most effective way to combat any risks of Measles, Mumps, or Rubella.

In an interesting turn of events, while partisanship correlates with whether an individual is likely to vaccinate, it doesn’t correlate as cleanly for a community. Upon conducting an analysis of the data between counties that reported their data, and the results of the 2024 presidential election, there was no statistically significant evidence to suggest that counties that voted in the direction of the Republican Ticket were less likely to be vaccinated. While there was an incredibly small correlation between counties that have a red-leaning and lower vaccination rates, it was negligible at best.

On an individual level, more left-leaning voters tend to be more vaccinated in comparison to right-leaning voters. But on a community scale, the downward trend of vaccination is troubling. There are a few explanations for this.

One is that RFK’s rhetoric has only recently become a larger part of the Trump coalition. Prior to that, Trump’s HHS Secretary, Alex Azar, told many reporters about the importance of the MMR vaccine, while also heading Project Warp Speed, the initiative that generated the COVID-19 vaccine. The data analyzed was from 2024, prior to the appointment of RFK, and thus still evolving.

Another reason is to consider that these are views in the minority opinion. Take, for example, the source of the outbreak this year, Gaines County, Texas. There, the vaccination rate was around 82%, meaning that 18% of the population opted not to vaccinate their children. While Gaines County has a majority of its population vaccinated, in order to tank a community’s vaccination rate, all you need is a small but noticeable 5.1% to elect not to vaccinate. Due to that minority growing across the nation, no matter the partisanship of the county, communities are losing that herd immunity.

Secretary Kennedy went on to write within the same op-ed that “the choice to vaccinate is a personal”, and did not mention anything to dissuade readers from his previously rebuked claims that there is a link between vaccines and autism. As the choice for vaccination remains with the public to decide, researchers such as Dr. Mathew Kaing of Stanford worry that if rates remain at the same level, measles “may become an endemic within 20 years”, let alone if they continue their downward trend.
